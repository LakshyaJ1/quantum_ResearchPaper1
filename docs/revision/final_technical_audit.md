## Starting-version methods clarifications, subsequently resolved

**Medium, reproducibility detail: disclose the subsecond setup parameter and exact step chronology.** The starting manuscript says one-second steps and explains atomic debit and deadline follow-up, but omits the selected 0.35-second online G3/G4 setup delay. `revision/scenarios.py:220` assigns 0.35 seconds to online fallback and zero to ideal pre-positioned material. `qkdsim/sim.py:433-436` checks the resulting completion timestamp before service; `qkdsim/sim.py:564-571` advances environmental state, generates one step of key, draws Poisson arrival counts, and then serves in priority/FIFO order. Consequently this is a discrete-time approximation with arrivals timestamped on the step grid, rather than an exact continuous-time Poisson event simulation. State these details together, describing the setup value as a scenario assumption rather than measured KEM latency. The current limitation excluding in-flight failures correctly scopes the fact that a successful attempt is committed immediately with a potentially later completion timestamp. A concise chronology also makes clear that a quantum-key debit is available for checking after that step's replenishment.

**Low, attack-observation ambiguity: identify which route events are counted and their window.** Starting manuscript line 151 says the online selector uses past committed-route counts. In fact `experiments/revision_online_adversary.py:113-127` excludes G4 service and records only successful routes with positive quantum-key consumption. `select_online_targets` at lines 129-152 uses those counts, and the update at lines 163-174 clears them after each 60-second selection. The observations therefore cover the preceding update window, not cumulative lifetime traffic and not every computational-fallback request. Replace the general phrase with successful quantum-key-consuming route commits during the previous 60 seconds. Retain the current statement that only transport reachability is additionally visible and that unobserved viable paths receive weight 0.01. The greedy coverage-per-cost choice is an explicit heuristic, not a proof of optimal interdiction.

**Low, metric edge-case clarity: distinguish undefined ratios from zero loss.** The equations require nonzero denominators. `revision/metrics.py:11-12` returns NaN for an empty cohort; artifact serialization maps undefined values to JSON null. LOG has no G1/G2-entitled arrivals by construction, so its entitled-loss fraction is undefined rather than zero. Starting manuscript lines 70-72 already exclude LOG from the entitled denominator. One added sentence stating that empty-denominator outcomes remain undefined would close the remaining reporting edge case. This does not affect any aggregate numerical result audited here.

## Verified interpretations and limits to preserve

The debit arithmetic is correct: with 240 metadata bits and 127 bits per authentication charge, G2 costs 750, 1385 and 5576 bits per hop at charge counts two, seven and forty. The reduced-load floor demand is 0.05(1385) + 0.002(145129) + 0.03(1257) = 397.218 bits/s across a cut traversed once by each request. Comparing that with 480 bits/s for two 240-bit/s branches is a nominal necessary capacity check, not proof of queue stability, finite-buffer reliability or feasible per-route allocation. The manuscript currently makes that distinction.

The finite cut bound remains a necessary upper bound on compliant consumption, because relay traffic must debit at least one crossing store and nonnegative remaining stock, overflow, purge and competing traffic can only lower usable resources. It is not generally attainable because stores cannot be freely pooled. The completion horizon must remain aligned with the bound: the simulator stops arrivals after 24 hours but follows pending requests to their deadlines, and a 24-hour attack releases before some late completions. The manuscript currently discloses this distinction and correctly attributes the nonzero transport-cut service entirely to post-release follow-up.

The exponent and abort-response model is a reduced engineering surrogate. Positive nominal generation, cumulative stock, authentication demand and expiry-free key assumptions drive these results; no composable finite-key proof, implemented hybrid combiner or deployed payload protection is established. G2 denotes ITS key delivery under trusted relays, while its associated payload encryption remains computational. The existing qualification of G1-G4 as an operator contract rather than a universal strength ordering should remain.

The runtime monitor, rather than training, guarantees the arrival-prefix exception cap under the simulator's sequential commit assumptions. These count caps do not bound payload-weighted exposure, and C2 service can still be blocked even with zero permitted C2 exceptions. The learning intervals condition on the selected frozen tables and seven fixed conditions; they do not quantify uncertainty over repeated policy training or all possible deployment conditions. The positive robust utility difference is small, and the adverse ISR and payload outcomes must remain visible.

The larger-stock null finding, unfavorable nominal-training regimes, and post-hoc status of the disjoint-seed persistence diagnostic are all materially important. Adding them did not change the original scope into a new photonics model, cryptographic proof or routing-algorithm claim. The online persistence result identifies a change in realized suppression exposure; it should not be generalized to an intrinsic advantage of adaptive attackers. Literature-derived arithmetic checks and capacity envelopes should likewise remain labelled as parameter/provenance checks, rather than hardware validation of network predictions.
