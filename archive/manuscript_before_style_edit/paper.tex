\begin{abstract}
In multi-hop trusted-relay quantum key distribution (QKD), secret key is a
depletable per-link resource, since every hop of a relayed path consumes it. How a
network should behave when it runs short is unsettled: ITU-T Y.3803 permits
degradation to a symmetric cipher, whereas a recent layered design fails closed,
treating the transition as a downgrade attack. We quantify the trade-off on an
eight-node tactical relay formation and four further graphs with minimum cuts of two
to four links, under an adversary that raises the quantum bit error rate until
targeted links abort key generation. We introduce \emph{forced assurance loss}---the
fraction of requests entitled to an information-theoretically secure (ITS) key that
are served without one---and the \emph{downgrade-forcing cost}, the adversary effort
required to induce it. Holding the formation's two-link cut for 24 hours, with stores at 2\% of usable capacity, drops fail-closed service from 97.5\% to 6.7\%.
Fail-degraded operation keeps 97.5\% of requests served, but only 6.6\% at their
authorised grade, and forces 90.1\% of entitled requests below an ITS key. Forcing
needs a per-link capability, a cost-weighted cut and endurance: with full stores the
same attack forces nothing within 24 hours. A learned grade controller, tested on
held-out seeds, gains little utility and raises below-ITS payload exposure. The
assurance-grade composition of served traffic belongs in QKD resilience reporting.
\end{abstract}

Quantum key distribution, trusted-relay networks, key management, post-quantum

\section{Introduction}
A brigade in mountainous terrain reaches its rear area through a chain of relay
sites, carrying command circuits rekeyed every few minutes, imagery encrypted at
one-time-pad rates, and bulk delay-tolerant logistics. Suppose the network can no
longer supply quantum key for all of it. Refusing service shows the commander an
outage that can be acted on; holding the circuit up and rekeying the imagery link
with a computationally secure algorithm shows nothing, and the loss stays invisible
until audit. Which failure is preferable is not settled.

Trusted-relay operation makes the question sharp. Beyond one quantum channel's
reach, keys extend hop by hop, each node re-encrypting the end-to-end key under its
neighbour key with a one-time pad and an ITS authenticator~\cite{salvail}. An
$n$-hop path therefore consumes key on \emph{every} one of its $n$ links, and at
free-space per-hop rates of order $10^{2}$\,bit/s the key supply binds, not the
existence of a quantum channel.

Standards, key managers and layered designs resolve exhaustion in opposing ways. One
permits degradation: a symmetric cipher may substitute for unavailable one-time-pad
key if the method is recorded~\cite{itu3803}; deployments do so~\cite{makris}, and
key managers can put both branches under policy control~\cite{tysowski}. The other
fails closed, tearing down the tunnel to prevent a \emph{downgrade
attack}~\cite{spooren}. The concern predates the mechanism: an adversary unable to
steal quantum keys may deny them instead, precisely to \emph{force} classical
cryptography into use~\cite{schartner}.

\textbf{The gap.} Neither branch has been measured on a multi-hop network, and we
know of no study of what either costs, or what an adversary must spend to force the
choice. The modes hand the adversary different currencies---refusal converts attack
effort into visible outage, degradation into silent assurance loss---so an operator
choosing between them chooses blind.

\textbf{Contributions.} (i) A threat-aware model in which stored key outlives
generation, with three separate failure surfaces (\S\ref{sec:link}); (ii)
\emph{forced assurance loss} on traffic \emph{entitled} to ITS key beside
floor-compliant service, the \emph{downgrade-forcing cost} with per-link attack
costs, and a cut condition predicting service under a held cut
(\S\ref{sec:metrics}); (iii) evidence on five graphs that forcing needs capability, a
cut and endurance, with routing and retargeting analyses
(\S\ref{sec:head}--\S\ref{sec:sens}); (iv) a learned grade controller audited on
disjoint seeds (\S\ref{sec:audit}); (v) an accounting check against published
measurements (\S\ref{sec:valid}).

Trusted-relay QKD research follows four largely independent lines. Routing work
asks which path a request should take under key
scarcity~\cite{vanduijn,johann,hao,cvitic}, and quality-of-service work adds
priority classes and admission control~\cite{mehic} or couples key supply to an
optical control plane~\cite{cao}. Both share one outcome model, a path with key on
every hop or a blocked request, so priority decides \emph{which} requests receive
quantum key, not \emph{what kind}.

The third line addresses exhaustion. Standards and key managers permit recorded
substitution~\cite{itu3803,tysowski}, deployments implement it~\cite{makris}, graded
delivery has been specified~\cite{sanz} and costed~\cite{zhu}, and a layered design
fails closed~\cite{spooren}. Single-link studies trigger fallback to a KEM on attack
or failure~\cite{agalya,hernandez} but score it by availability, which counts a
post-quantum-only session as served. The fourth line models an
adversary---key-store depletion by denial of service~\cite{mehicdos}, attack-radius
minimisation~\cite{li}, zero-trust relays~\cite{ghorab}, swept degradation for
6G~\cite{hafeez}---yet keeps the served-or-blocked outcome, so the
force-classical-crypto threat~\cite{schartner} is unrepresented.

Table~\ref{tab:pos} covers studies that model key supply across more than one relay
hop. Its assurance column holds no full mark: none we located reports the fraction of
\emph{entitled} traffic served below ITS, \cite{zhu} being nearest but neither
restricted to entitled traffic nor swept. To our knowledge no prior work compares the
two fail modes on a multi-hop network, or prices what forcing the choice costs an
adversary.

\section{System Model}
Since \emph{hybrid} denotes key-combiner composition secure if one component
survives~\cite{ietfhybrid}, a QKD$\rightarrow$post-quantum substitution is not
hybrid; we call it a labelled grade downgrade.

\centering

\caption{(a) The eight-node formation, per-link secret key rates and the minimum cut isolating its forward posts. (b) The three failure surfaces, which the model keeps
separate (\S\ref{sec:link}).}
\label{fig:arch}

We model (T1) an eight-node formation (rear node, two relays, two brigade nodes,
three forward posts; one 300\,kbit/s fibre and eight 200--400\,bit/s free-space
links, Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}a) and (T2) a depth-$d$ chain with one longer alternate for $d\ge2$. An eight-node ring, a $3\times3$ grid and 3- and 4-path parallel graphs, each with one source and one sink and 240\,bit/s links, add minimum cuts of two, two, three and four. Sessions run
from the rear node to the forward posts and debit every hop's reservoir; every
formation session crosses one of two 240\,bit/s links, so the fibre's surplus is
stranded one hop from the bottleneck.

Reservoirs follow QKDNetSim's key-buffer semantics with its SECOQC example
values~\cite{qkdnetsim}: capacity $52\,428\,800$, threshold $11\,324\,620$ and a
protected reserve of $1\,048\,576$ bits, the role SECOQC gave to key held for
authentication~\cite{peev}. Overflow is discarded; we found no published capacity from vendors or ETSI~\cite{etsi014}. Distilled key stays spendable when generation stops, so
an attack on the quantum channel removes replenishment, not stock; stores start at a
declared usable fill, 2\% (1.03\,Mbit per link) in the main runs.

Sessions are served at four grades: \GI\ one-time pad (key $=$ payload bits);
\GII\ QKD-keyed symmetric (256\,bit, ITS-\emph{delivered} key under a computational
cipher); \GIII\ hybrid QKD\,$\oplus$\,ML-KEM-768 (128\,bit plus KEM); \GIV\
ML-KEM-768 only. Each class carries an \emph{authorised floor}; unmet, the
controller blocks at the deadline (\textsc{fail\_closed}) or serves below the floor
at once and records an auditable violation (\textsc{fail\_degraded}).

\subsection{Link model and adversary}\label{sec:link}
An adversary raising noise past the security threshold halts key
generation~\cite{alleaume}, so a fixed degraded rate is indefensible. Links are
\textsc{nominal} ($R_0$), \textsc{degraded} ($R_0\rho(e)$), in \textsc{abort} (no
fresh key, $e\ge e_{\mathrm{abort}}$) or in \textsc{outage} (no classical
transport), with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rho}
\rho(e)=\left[\frac{\max\!\left(0,\,1-(1+f)H_2(e)\right)}
{1-(1+f)H_2(e_0)}\right]^{\gamma},
\end{equation}
$f=1.16$ and $e_0=0.03$~\cite{sasaki}. We report $\gamma=1$ (asymptotic BB84) and $\gamma=3.4$, an
illustrative steeper shape matched to one datapoint from another
modality~\cite{sasaki}, not a calibration; $e_{\mathrm{abort}}=0.075$ is an
engineering value (the NEC--NICT engine of~\cite{sasaki} codes only below 7.5\%),
with 0.11 as a sensitivity.

QBER pressure stops generation but leaves the classical channel, and so relaying of
stored key and any fallback, intact; classical-channel denial halts post-processing
and transport~\cite{mehic}; denial of the online key-establishment service blocks a
KEM fallback but not QKD-keyed grades or pre-placed keys (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}b).
Detector blinding~\cite{lydersen} raises no QBER and is invisible here. The adversary
injects excess QBER $\Delta e$ on the links it buys~\cite{schartner}, at relative cost
1 per free-space link and 10 for the fibre (1 and 100 as sensitivities), scenario
weights rather than measured expenditure. It targets by static betweenness per unit
cost, \emph{residual} betweenness recomputed after each removal, the cost-weighted
minimum cut, or online from committed routes.

\subsection{Key accounting and traffic}\label{sec:acct}
Relaying $K$ bits of key across one hop costs
\begin{equation}\label{eq:debit}
q=K+M_{\mathrm{meta}}+n_{\mathrm{auth}}\,t,
\end{equation}
with metadata $M_{\mathrm{meta}}=240$\,bit (Y.3803 relays a key file) and tag
$t=127$\,bit~\cite{walenta}, charged per authenticated message~\cite{fung}. The count $n_{\mathrm{auth}}$ per delivery has no specification: 2 is a lower bound and field data imply about 7 (\S\ref{sec:valid}), so results use 7 unless stated (a \GII\ debit of 1385\,bit per hop), with 2 and 40 as sensitivities.

Demands follow measured deployments. C2: 2048\,bit, floor \GII, 5\,s deadline,
$\lambda=0.05$\,s$^{-1}$ (256\,bit/min per encryptor~\cite{stucki}); ISR:
144\,000\,bit, floor \GI, 30\,s, a 60\,s session of 2.4\,kbps one-time-pad
voice~\cite{chen}; LOG: 320\,000\,bit~\cite{chen}, floor \GIII, 120\,s,
$\lambda=0.03$\,s$^{-1}$; priority follows that order. At 0.02\,s$^{-1}$ (ISR factor 1) the mean debit across the formation's cut would exceed six times its 480\,bit/s supply, a class nothing could fund, so ISR runs at factor 0.1, 0.002\,s$^{-1}$ (397\,bit/s across the cut, 83\% of supply), with factors 1 (overload) and 0.03 (light) as sensitivities.

\subsection{Metrics and a cut condition}\label{sec:metrics}
Of $N$ arriving requests, let $S$ be served within their deadlines and $D\subseteq S$
below their authorised floor:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:metrics}
A=|S|/N,\qquad Q=|S\setminus D|/N,\qquad L=|D|/N,
\end{equation}
so $A=Q+L$: availability splits into compliant service $Q$ and below-floor service $L$, which carries post-quantum-only fallback. \emph{Forced assurance loss} $F=|D_{\mathrm{ITS}}|/|E|$ divides the
requests entitled to ITS key (floors \GI, \GII) that are served at \GIII\ or \GIV\ by all entitled arrivals, blocked ones included, so both modes share one denominator; an ISR request served at \GII\
misses its floor (counted in $L$) but keeps an ITS-delivered key (not in $F$).
Payload weighting gives $F_B$, and class weights $(5,2,1)$ and $(10,3,1)$
for (C2, ISR, LOG) a second reweighting, both exposure proxies.

For a cut $\delta(W)$ separating the rear node from the forward posts, with usable
stock $U_e(0)$ and generation $G_e(T)$ on cut link $e$, every compliant session
completed by $T$ debits some cut link at least its floor debit $q_s$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cut}
\textstyle\sum_{s\in\mathcal{C}_T} q_s\;\le\;\sum_{e\in\delta(W)}\bigl[U_e(0)+G_e(T)\bigr].
\end{equation}
Holding every cut link past abort sets $G_e=0$, so at demand
$\bar D=\sum_c\lambda_cq_c$ compliant service lasts at most $T^\ast=\sum_eU_e(0)/\bar
D$, 1.44\,h for the formation at 2\% fill. The condition is necessary, not
sufficient: a test graph of three two-hop branches strands 400 bits per branch and
serves six requests where it permits seven.

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}
Discrete-event simulation at 1\,s resolution: admission checks every hop before any
debit, a pending request retries until its deadline, and \GIII/\GIV\ need a 0.35\,s
online setup that must fit it. Arrivals and the attack run for 24\,h, and pending
requests are followed to their deadlines. A declared initial fill replaces a warm-up, since full stores drain only under an overloaded ISR class. Free-space
generation and every link's transport fail independently (mean up 3600\,s, down
120\,s); a severe-weather variant (900/600\,s) runs independently or switches all free-space generation together. \emph{Common random numbers} give every policy and attack identical arrivals and environmental draws per seed, so attack effects are paired differences against the unattacked run. We report means of seedwise fractions with
95\% percentile bootstrap intervals (20\,000 resamples) over twelve seeds (main grid, routing, floors, code comparison), eight (sensitivities) or six (online adversary, abort sweep).

\subsection{Validation}\label{sec:valid}
No established simulator models a trusted-relay key economy (SeQUeNCe, QuISP and
NetSquid have no key pool; QKDNetSim, whose buffers we adopt, no admission
control), and no deployment publishes the assurance composition of its traffic. A
43-check gate and 127 regression tests with exact resource fixtures precede every
result, and four published measurements test the accounting. \textbf{V1} (SECOQC
rerouting~\cite{peev}) matches to the second, which is arithmetic consistency,
not validation; \textbf{V4} bounds the one-time-pad demand law to an order of
magnitude (1.2--12.6 voice pairs against eleven~\cite{chen}). \textbf{V2 and V3 fail,
in the same direction:} relaying cost Cambridge $3.69\times$ the delivered
key~\cite{dynes} against our $2.23\times$ at $n_{\rm auth}=2$, and SECOQC's tunnel
spent 91.2\% of its rekey budget on authentication overhead against our 65.9\% (metadata and tags). Solved for
$n_{\rm auth}$ they give $\approx7$ per relay hop and $\approx40$ per tunnel; neither
pins the count, but both fix the bias, hence our default of 7.

The transition is a cliff (Table~\ref{tab:head}). Holding the formation's two-link
cut for 24\,h collapses fail-closed service from 97.5\% to 6.7\%, while
fail-degraded operation still serves 97.5\%---but only 6.6\% at the authorised

requests below an ITS key (CI $[89.2,91.0]$; payload-weighted 90.3\%), almost all
at \GIV, since the \GIII\ hybrid still needs 1257\,bit of quantum key per hop.

One cut link shows a second mechanism. Fail-closed service falls 4.6 points, and ISR, whose 145\,kbit debits no longer fit one 240\,bit/s link, loses half its service; fail-degraded
operation forces 4.8\% below ITS, and its compliant service falls 1.2 points
\emph{below} fail-closed's (CI $[-1.4,-1.1]$), because it downgrades at once requests
that waiting would have served. A static-betweenness adversary buys BDE1--BDE2 and BDE1--REL1, isolating FWD1 and FWD2 but not FWD3, and forces 59.0\%; its first link alone costs fail-closed service 0.7 points.

\textbf{Both losses are near zero unattacked.} $F$ is zero under fail-closed and 0.002\% under fail-degraded operation (one request in twelve runs), and ISR reaches its one-time-pad floor in 97.0\% of arrivals, against 12.6\% at factor 1: a \GI\ floor fails through overload, not by construction.

(Fig.~\ref{fig:scale}). With the budget equal to the cut, $F$ is 87.6--92.9\% on all
five graphs, and fail-closed service is the stock behind the cut divided by the
demand: $kU/(\bar DT)$ predicts 6.0, 9.0 and 12.0\% for two-, three- and four-link cuts
against 6.5--6.7, 9.6 and 12.3\% observed, slightly above because the stock serves cheaper requests once ISR's large debits stop fitting. With one cut link suppressed, loss
appears only where the remaining links cannot carry the 397\,bit/s demand:
4.8--11.5\% on two-link cuts, 0.71 and 0.05\% otherwise.

\subsection{Key-aware routing under a fallback}\label{sec:routing}
\textbf{(1) Availability becomes degenerate; compliant service does not.} A
post-quantum fallback succeeds wherever a classical path exists, so unattacked
key-aware and shortest-path routing give identical fail-degraded availability in
every seed and condition: the zero-width interval is an identity of the model, not a measured precision.
$Q$ still discriminates: key-aware routing raises it by 0.12 points (CI
$[0.05,0.19]$) for 5.0\,Mbit (CI $[2.3,7.7]$) more key per day.

\textbf{(2) The hop penalty is a warm-up transient.} At ISR factor 1, key-aware routing loses 2.6 points of fail-closed service to shortest path (CI $[-2.9,-2.3]$) only when measured after a 12\,h warm-up from full stores, where it has left less stock for ISR; measured from $t=0$ it gains 0.08 points, from 2\% fill 0.11--0.13, and at the rescaled load 0.13 (CI $[0.07,0.20]$).
The hop multiplier shows in expenditure, not service; reported 25--40\%
gains~\cite{cvitic} measure blocking without a fallback.

\textbf{(3) Reservation floors are an admission decision, not a graded control.}
Class-aware floors~\cite{mehic}, reserving 20 and 40\% of each store against ISR and
LOG, deny logistics its quantum key at 2\% fill (fail-closed service $-37.6$ points,
logistics 98.9$\rightarrow$0\%), and fail-degraded operation then forces 2.1\% below
ITS with no adversary present; at full stock and the rescaled load they are inert. Under the cut, denial buys endurance, $+14.3$ points of fail-closed service (CI $[13.4,15.2]$) and
$F$ down from 90.1 to 63.6\%, because logistics' hybrid debits stop draining the cut.

\subsection{Downgrade-forcing cost versus depth}\label{sec:cost}
\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig3_forcing_cost.pdf}
\caption{Forcing cost $k^\ast$, the smallest budget whose mean forced loss reaches
50\% in 24\,h, against chain depth (fail-degraded, 2\% fill, eight seeds; online

\label{fig:cost}

For $d\ge2$ every link of the depth-$d$ chain lies on one cycle with equal betweenness, so a static centrality ranking is decided
by its tie order: ours attacks the longer alternate first and needs $d+2$ links to
force half the entitled traffic below ITS (another tie order needs $d+1$).
Recomputing betweenness on the residual graph after each removal needs the minimum cut, two links, at every depth $d\ge2$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:cost}). An online adversary that sees only committed routes and updates every 60\,s holds a cut from its first choice if it keeps its slots (89.7--92.9\% at depths 2, 4 and 6; 48 link-hours); if it releases slots while its path is momentarily unreachable, forced loss falls with depth from 72.3 to 35.5\%, as longer paths are unreachable more often and released links refill. Linear growth was a property of a static ranking, which overstates resilience: against residual betweenness or a cut-holding adversary depth buys no protection. Per-link costs matter on the
formation: with the fibre 10 or 100 times dearer, residual betweenness avoids it and forces 10.1\% at budget two, not 89.8\%; the fibre-free cut is unaffected.

\textbf{Capability.} A link at nominal $e_0$ aborts once $\Delta e\ge
e_{\mathrm{abort}}-e_0=0.045$; below that it keeps
$\rho(e)$ of its rate, and loss is forced once the cut's residual supply nears the entitled classes' cheapest ITS demand, about 72\,bit/s, plus the hybrid debits competing for it. The response shape
therefore matters below the threshold, not above it (Table~\ref{tab:envelope}). At
$\gamma=1$, sub-threshold injection forces under 0.1\% on the formation at any budget
up to five links; without outages, one link short of the cut, even supra-threshold injection forces under 0.1\% (4.8\% with them, Table~\ref{tab:head}); and every budget from the cut upward gives the same 93.1\%. The three-
and four-link cuts repeat the pattern. At $\gamma=3.4$
capability arrives before abort: $\Delta e=0.03$ leaves 9.8\% of the rate and forces
63.0, 41.6 and 17.3\% at the three cuts with no link aborted. Raising
$e_{\mathrm{abort}}$ to 0.11 moves the threshold to $\Delta e=0.068$, not the 0.08 that $e_{\mathrm{abort}}-e_0$ suggests, because the ITS zero of~(\ref{eq:rho}), at 9.8\%, binds
first; $\Delta e=0.06$ already forces 40.9\% on the three-link cut.

\textbf{Endurance.} Sensitivities use eight seeds (headline cell $F=89.9\%$, fail-closed $A=7.0\%$). Initial fills of 2, 10 and 100\% give $F=89.9$, 65.4 and 0\%, and 1, 6 and 24\,h attacks at 2\% fill 0.06, 17.6 and 89.9\%, first forcing after about 1.5\,h, the $T^\ast$ of~(\ref{eq:cut}). The same rule predicts 7.2 and 72\,h at 10 and 100\% fill, against a median first forcing of 6.6\,h and none within 24\,h. Stock matters from full stores too: at ISR factor 1 and $n_{\mathrm{auth}}=2$, after a 12\,h warm-up, treating an aborted link's stock as unusable leaves 30.4\% fail-closed service and $F=65.1\%$ under the two-link betweenness attack; spending it gives 76.7\% (paired $+46.4$ points, CI $[45.8,46.9]$) and 4.1\%. Forcing thus needs three things: a per-link capability, a
cost-weighted cut, and a duration the stock behind the cut cannot cover.

\textbf{The rescaled load.} Unattacked, the fail-mode gap
vanishes at sustainable load (paired $\Delta A=0.00$ and $+0.01$ points at ISR
factors 0.03 and 0.1) and appears only through overload ($+17.2$ at factor 1). Under the cut it persists at every load, narrowing only as a lighter load
drains the stock more slowly ($\Delta A=85.4$, 90.6 and 94.7 points), with $\Delta Q\le0$ throughout on the formation: it never inverts.

\textbf{Authentication.} With $n_{\mathrm{auth}}$ of 2 or 40 instead of 7,
fail-closed service under the cut is 8.5 or 3.3\% (7.0\%) and $F$ 87.9 or 93.5\%
(89.9\%); at 40 the unattacked formation already forces 3.7\%, so policy differences
do not cancel as a common term.

\textbf{Correlated weather.} Weather can sever the cut with no adversary: up/down
times of 900/600\,s give an unattacked $F$ of 1.7\% (independent links) and 3.0\%
(all free-space generation failing together) and lower unattacked fail-closed service to 95.4 and 94.2\%, while the paired attack excess stays at 88.1 and 86.9 points. $F$ counts loss whatever its cause, so attribution needs the
weather-matched baseline; a held correlated outage looks like an attack of the same duration.

\textbf{The fallback needs the classical channel.} Fail-degraded
availability survives QBER attack because aborted links keep their classical
channels; denying transport on the cut drops both modes to 0.06\%. Pre-placed
symmetric keys cannot change this, since ciphertext still needs a path; they remove
instead the dependence on a live key-establishment service: with it down under the held cut, an online KEM fallback serves 5.0\% and pre-placed keys 97.5\%, still forcing 89.9\% below
ITS. They relocate the dependency, not the assurance loss; their lifetime, refresh and distribution cost are not modelled, so their 97.5\% is an upper bound.

Trained across nominal, attacked and severe-weather runs, the learner gains $\Delta J=+1.1\times10^{-3}$; that the heuristic reaches 99.8\% of it reflects our
search budget, not the headroom. The gain spends ISR's allowance where it costs the
most one-time-pad payload (ISR $Q$ $-2.8$ points, $F_B$ $+1.7$), under every class
weighting. As $\Delta J$ is linear in the weights, reversal prices are algebra: the
heuristic beats the strict floor for $\lambda<0.98$ (CI $[0.96,1.00]$) in
$J=A-\lambda L$, while the learned win is insensitive to $\lambda$ and reverses at a
forced-loss price $\mu^\ast=0.32$ (CI $[0.06,0.60]$; $\Delta F=+0.33$ points); the
search only supplies the candidate. Tables trained on nominal traffic alone lose, all
on the stressed conditions, because nominal runs never reach depletion, a failure
invisible from training curves; and the per-class shield is spent where scoring is
cheapest, so floors must be specified \emph{and} priced per class.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
Choosing between refusing and degrading chooses which resource the adversary takes. Operationally, three rules follow: report $Q$ and $F$ beside $A$; provision stock behind the minimum cut for $T^\ast$ hours of the attacks of concern; and set fail modes and exception allowances per class, pricing each allowance. Limitations: key rates are parameterised, not simulated
photonics; response shapes are illustrative; relays are trusted; detector blinding
is invisible by construction; weights are exposure proxies; fill, outages and attack
costs are declared scenarios, swept rather than assumed; no cipher or KEM is
executed; the policy search was modest; the five graphs are synthetic. Next:
QKDNetSim cross-validation and a reimplemented published router. Y.3803 asks
key managers to record the relay method and prefer OTP-relayed keys for QKD-level
requests~\cite{itu3803}, but the ETSI interface~\cite{etsi014} still exposes no grade
a consumer could enforce.

\section{Conclusion}
Standards permit fallback to weaker key supply; a layered post-quantum design
forbids it as a downgrade attack. Holding the formation's minimum cut for a day
either collapses fail-closed service from 97.5\% to 6.7\%, or keeps 97.5\% served
while only 6.6\% meets its authorised grade and 90.1\% of entitled traffic is forced
below ITS protection; on all five graphs a held cut forces 87.6--92.9\%. Forcing requires a per-link capability, a cost-weighted cut and a duration the stock behind the cut cannot cover. A fallback also needs the classical channel, and a learned controller buys its small gain with one-time-pad payload. We argue that the assurance-grade composition of \emph{served} traffic belongs in QKD resilience reporting alongside key-supply continuity.
